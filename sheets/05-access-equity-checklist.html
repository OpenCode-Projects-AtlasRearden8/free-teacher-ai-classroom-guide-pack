Access Equity Checklist — Choose Your Own

This is a menu, not a mandate. Before your first AI lesson, skim each category, check the items you can commit to this term, and — just as important — pick one fallback per category for the students who need one. A class-wide AI plan is only fair if every student can actually reach the tool, the internet, and the language it speaks.

The one rule that matters

For every AI activity you assign, there must be some non-AI way to earn full credit for the same thinking. Check the Fallbacks boxes below for what that looks like in practice.

Category A — Devices and Internet

I know which students have home internet and which don't (school survey or quick morning-show-of-hands check).
Every AI activity I assign can be completed during class time or library hours — nothing requires at-home access.
Students without personal devices can share one, use a school device, or use paper printouts of AI sample answers.
Fallback: I keep printed example AI outputs (good ones and flawed ones) so a no-internet student can still complete the AI-Included analysis work.
Fallback: For AI-Assisted practice, my non-connected alternative is handwritten brainstorming with a partner or with me in study hall.

Category B — Language Support

Translation and reading-support tools are allowed on every assignment, including AI-Free work, and stay separate from the AI misconduct conversation entirely.
Students with a different home language know they can use translation to read or discuss, even when AI can't write their final work.
Key instructions on AI behavior exist in families' home languages (or a colleague can translate them).
Fallback: My non-AI translation alternative is bilingual glossary sheets, peer translation with a classmate, or my ESL co-teacher's support — either is acceptable.

Category C — Disability and Accessibility

Students with IEP/504 accommodations, assistive tech, or accessibility needs know those tools are always allowed and always unaffected by any AI-Free label.
Any AI platform I use in class is screen-reader friendly, keyboard-navigable, or has an alternative access route — I verify this once before week one.
Text size, contrast, and reading time in AI printouts or transcripts are adjustable for low-vision or slower-processing students.
Fallback: If the AI platform is frustrating for a particular student, my alternative is a human peer, my read-aloud, or a paper-based version of the same thinking.

Category D — Time, Support, and Cost

Nothing in my AI plan requires a paid subscription, an app students download at home, or money spent by families.
Students caring for siblings, working jobs, or with limited evening study time can complete AI-Assisted assignments in class or during designated support periods.
There's a quick way for any student to ask me (or a trusted adult) "Am I allowed to use AI on this?" instead of guessing.
Fallback: If a student misses the AI lesson entirely (absence, mid-year transfer, etc.), my catch-up version is a 10-minute one-on-one walkthrough with the same printed materials.

Category E — Privacy and Data

Students under 13 (or per your platform's minimum age) have an adult-approved account or a teacher-demo account rather than their own.
Nobody — student or teacher — pastes real names, grades, photos, health information, or full personal essays into public AI tools.
I know my district's rules on AI platforms and student data before assigning anything that requires an account.
Fallback: If a family opts out of account-based AI entirely, my alternative is printed transcripts, my live demos, and the paper reflections in this pack — the full thinking, no account needed.
