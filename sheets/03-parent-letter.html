Sheet 3

Parent Letter Drafts

Families mostly want two things: reassurance that AI isn't replacing their child's thinking, and a way to reach you with questions. Both drafts below deliver exactly that. Fill in every  -marked blank, cut whichever paragraphs you don't need, and send. No tech vocabulary required to understand them.

Draft 1 — Short note (good for a newsletter or one-page flyer)

Dear families,

This year our class will sometimes use AI tools — the kind of apps that answer questions, draft text, or help students practice — when they genuinely support learning. It happens only when we've talked about it in advance, it's never used on assignments marked "AI-Free," and students are asked to describe honestly how any AI tool helped their work. On some assignments we use AI as the object of study itself: critiquing its answers, spotting its mistakes, comparing its writing to our own.

Our aim is simple: by the time they graduate, students can use AI thoughtfully — and distrust it confidently — without outsourcing the thinking we actually grade.

Warmly,
 

Draft 2 — Full letter (start of term, with the opt-out line)

Dear families of [Student Name],

Welcome to [Course Name]. One of the questions families ask me early each year is how AI tools like chatbots fit into our classroom — so I'm writing before school starts rather than after a rumor spreads.

What we'll do. AI tools can quiz a student for practice, explain a step they missed, or suggest how an argument might be organized — the way a study partner might — and we'll use them on assignments where that kind of support genuinely helps learning. On work that measures what your child can do independently, we mark it AI-Free and use nothing, so those assessments stay fair. When AI help is allowed, students attach a short honest note about what they used and how. And periodically we do the opposite: we take an AI's answer, critique it, find its mistakes, and rewrite it. That teaches skepticism — the skill your child will actually need.

What we won't do. We won't use AI to grade, recommend tracks, or contact families on our behalf. We won't enter any student's grades, name, or other personal information into public AI tools. Any AI tool used in class is chosen because it supports a lesson we've already designed — not the other way around.

What counts as "cheating." If an assignment doesn't permit AI, using it — including "just rewriting" a borrowed paragraph past recognition — follows our standard academic honesty process, the same as copying another student's work. First small cases are usually a redo-with-disclosure, not a referral; I care far more about teaching the habit of honesty than about catching anyone.

Your part. You don't need to supervise AI at all — that's my job during class. If you're curious what your child is working on, ask them to show you their reflection sheet: a page where they describe, in their words, whether and how AI helped. It's been surprisingly honest reading in past years.

If you'd prefer your child not use AI tools in my class for any reason, please reply to this note or email me at   and I'll arrange a no-AI version of every affected assignment — no questions or forms needed. Students with school-issued accessibility devices or translation tools are never affected by any of this; those supports stay in place regardless of assignment type.

Thank you for letting me work with your student this year.

Sincerely,
 
[Course Name] · [School Name] · [Room Number or Days/Times]

Worth reading twice before sending

The sentence about accessibility supports ("never affected by any of this") resolves the most common family anxiety we see with AI policies — that support tools get swept up in bans. Keep it in whichever draft you send.

Possible family questions and short answers

If they ask…A fair, plain-English reply
"Isn't AI making kids lazier?"Sometimes, which is why assignments say exactly when it's allowed. Independent, AI-Free work is still the backbone of the grade.
"How do you know they're not cheating?"Trusted in-class checkpoints, drafts, and reflection sheets — describing someone else's process in detail is hard to fake.
"Which AI products do you use?"Naming tools invites comparison and marketing — I choose what fits the lesson and reprint this letter annually with the current list if it changes.
"Can I see what my child wrote?"Always — their reflection sheet, drafts, and completed work are all open to you.
"My child already uses AI daily."Great — then the class's job is even more relevant: learning when to trust it, when to check it, and how to describe their own honest process.

Both drafts mention no specific product names so they don't age out of date and never imply endorsement. If your school standardizes on a particular tool, you can name it in the "What we'll do" paragraph.
